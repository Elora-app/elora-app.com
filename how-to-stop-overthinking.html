✦ ELORA GUIDE

How to Stop
Overthinking.

You're not too much. Your mind is just tired.

"Overthinking isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when a sensitive mind tries to feel safe in an uncertain world."

First — Understand What's Actually Happening

Overthinking is your brain trying to protect you. It replays conversations to find what went wrong. It imagines worst case scenarios to prepare you. It searches for certainty because uncertainty feels dangerous.

It's not weakness. It's a mind that cares deeply — and hasn't yet learned that it doesn't need to solve everything to be safe.

The goal isn't to stop thinking. It's to stop believing every thought is true, urgent, or worth your full attention.

What Actually Helps

1. Write it down — all of it

The most effective thing you can do when your mind won't stop is get everything out of your head and onto paper. Not to analyze it. Just to empty it.

When thoughts are swirling in your head, they feel enormous. When you write them down, they become manageable. They're just words on a page — not the entire world.

TRY THISSet a timer for 5 minutes. Write everything in your head without stopping or editing. When the timer goes off, close the journal. You've left it there. You don't have to carry it anymore.

2. Separate facts from the story you're adding

Overthinking is almost always about the story we add to what happened — not what actually happened.

Something happens (fact). Then your brain adds meaning to it (story). The fact is neutral. The story is where the suffering lives.

ASK YOURSELF"What actually happened?" vs "What am I telling myself it means?" Write both down. The gap between them is where most of your anxiety lives.

3. Focus on what's in your control

Most overthinking is about things we can't control — other people's opinions, the future, outcomes. You can think about those things forever and nothing will change.

When your mind goes there, redirect: what can I actually do right now? Even one small action breaks the overthinking loop.

4. Give your thoughts a time limit

Instead of trying to stop thinking entirely (which doesn't work), give yourself a designated worry time. 15 minutes, once a day. Overthink everything you want during that window. When it's over, you're done.

This sounds strange but it actually works — it trains your brain that there's a time for this, and that time isn't now.

5. Interrupt the loop physically

Overthinking is a mental loop. The fastest way to break a mental loop is to change something physical — stand up, go outside, splash cold water on your face, put on music and move. Your body and mind are connected. Move one to shift the other.

6. Notice — don't fight

Fighting your thoughts makes them louder. Instead of "stop thinking about this," try: "I notice I'm thinking about this again." That tiny shift — from being inside the thought to observing it — creates distance. And distance creates peace.

THE SHIFTInstead of "Why can't I stop thinking about this?" try "I'm having the thought that..." and then complete the sentence. You're not the thought. You're the one noticing it.

7. Accept that uncertainty is safe

At the root of most overthinking is a belief that if you just think hard enough, you can figure out what's going to happen — and then you'll feel safe. But certainty is not actually available. And peace that depends on certainty is not actually peace.

The practice is learning to feel safe even when you don't know what's going to happen. That's not naive — it's the only way to actually stop overthinking.

For the Hard Nights

Overthinking is worst at night because there are no distractions. The thoughts that you managed to outrun during the day finally catch up to you.

BEFORE BEDWrite down: everything on your mind, one thing you're grateful for, and one thing you're going to do tomorrow. This empties your head, shifts your perspective, and gives you something to move toward.

And if the thoughts still come — let them. Don't fight. Just breathe, and remind yourself: "Not every thought needs a response tonight."

"You've survived 100% of your worst nights so far. Your mind was trying to protect you. Thank it — and then tell it to rest."
